9. Case Studies

Three worked examples that pull earlier chapters together into a finished piece. Each assumes you've read Getting Started; links point back to the chapter that covers each step in full.

Case study 1 — an 8-bar chiptune loop

Step-grid drums plus a Trine bassline, typical of a short overworld/menu loop for a game.

  1. Add a Step Grid Track named "Drums," create a one-bar Region, and program a kick/hat/snare pattern across a couple of lanes in the Beats window (see Tracks & Playlist). Drag the Region's right edge to loop it out to 8 bars.
  2. Add a Piano Roll Track named "Bass," switch its synth engine to Trine in its editor panel, and load a punchy bass preset from the Factory preset list (see Synth Engines). Draw a simple root-note bassline following your drum pattern's feel.
  3. Apply the Swing 8th (Light) groove template to the hi-hat lane and the bassline for a bit of chiptune bounce (see Groove & Tempo).
  4. Add a Bitcrusher at a low bit depth to the Drums track's FX chain for lo-fi grit, and a light Reverb on a send bus that both tracks feed a little into for glue (see Mixing & Effects).
  5. Set the loop count in File → Export… to however many repeats you need and bounce to WAV (see Files, MIDI & Export).

Case study 2 — comping a vocal take

Recording several passes of a short vocal line and combining the best parts of each into one clean take.

  1. Add an Audio Track — it's armed for recording automatically. Pick your input device and set the Trim knob so the level meter reads comfortably without clipping (see Files, MIDI & Export).
  2. Record a pass with ⏺ Record. Rewind the playhead back to the exact same start position and record again — the new pass joins the first as a second take in a Take Folder rather than replacing it. Repeat for a third pass.
  3. Double-click the Take Folder to open the comp editor. Listen through each take's lane and drag across the best-sounding stretch of each to paint together one composite performance (see Editing Audio).
  4. If one phrase drifted slightly off pitch, open Flex Time / Pitch… on the comped clip and nudge that note back in tune from the Pitch tab.
  5. Add a Noise Gate to clean up room noise between phrases, then a Compressor to even out dynamics, both on the track's own FX chain.

Case study 3 — a tempo-mapped boss-battle cue

A cue that starts at a tense, slower tempo and accelerates into a faster section when the fight intensifies — the kind of tempo change a tempo map exists for.

  1. Build the slow intro section (drums, a tense pad on a Wave-engine track, maybe a low drone) at whatever BPM feels right in the song's main tempo field.
  2. Open the Playlist's Tempo Track panel and insert a point at the tick where the fast section begins, then raise its BPM — everything after that point plays faster, everything before it is unaffected (see Groove & Tempo).
  3. Build the faster section's drums and lead on their own Regions positioned after that tempo point — since note/step triggering follows the tempo map exactly, they'll lock to the new tempo automatically.
  4. Add a Filter effect to the pad track and automate its cutoff with a Track Automation lane that opens up right around the tempo change, for a rising-tension sweep into the faster section (see Mixing & Effects).
  5. Automate the master bus's Limiter input gain (a Master FX automation target) to push a little harder once the fast section kicks in, for extra perceived loudness at the drop.
  6. Export, remembering that WAV export mixes every effect chain and automation lane exactly as heard live (see Files, MIDI & Export).
